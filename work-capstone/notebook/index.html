crime_outcomes = pd.concat(outcome_dfs, ignore_index=True)

2.2 - EDA Findings¶

Recorded crime is much higher in urban areas, accounting for $\approx 85\%$ of crimes, compared to $\approx 15\%$ in rural areas. This indicates a substancial imbalance in recorded crime volume, although the data is not adjusted for population size, Additionally, this could be indicative of potential under-reporting of rural crime as reported in literature.

Recorded crime types are broadly consistent when looking proportionally across Kent, although there are some clear distinctions between urban and rural areas. Shoplifting offences occur twice as much in urban areas, whilst there are marginally higher proportions of criminal damage and arson, vehicle crime, violence and sexual offences, and other crime in rural areas. Overall, violence and sexual offences dominate offences recorded, accounting for more than $40\%$ of crimes in both urban and rural areas.

Recorded crime outcomes are also broadly consistent across Kent. "Unable to prosecute suspect" and "Investigation complete; no suspect identified" dominate records, accounting for more than $70\%$ of outcomes in both urban and rural areas. "Suspect charged" accounts for $\approx 12\%$ of crimes in urban areas, however only $\approx 8\%$ of crimes in rural areas, potentially supporting literature claims that policing in rural areas is slightly less effective in terms of success rate. Outcomes "Investigation complete; no suspect identified" and "Action to be taken by another organistion" are slightly higher proprtionally in rural areas.

Raw counts show interesting insights. The 3 most recorded crime types in urab areas are violence and sexual offences, criminal damage and arson, and shoplifting. This differs to violence and sexual offences, criminal damage and arson and other theft for rural areas. The 3 most recorded crime outcomes in urban areas are "Unable to prosecute suspect", "Investigation complete; no suspect identified", and "suspect charged". This does not differ to rural areas.

2.4 - Chi-Squared Findings¶

Chi-squared independence tests suggest that crime type and outcome is independent of urban/rural classification, given the statistically significant p-value. However, chi-sqaured tests are senstive to large datasets so the results require further evaluation. Cramér’s V values < 0.11 for both crime type and outcome indicates whilst there exist some statistical differences, the overall disributions are broadly similar across rural and areas in Kent.

3.4 - Conclusion¶

Whilst appropriate for the dataset and task, the multinomial logistic regression model does not yield meaningful predictive performance for crime type. The initial model built using 2 features, 1 temporal/spatial, achieved an F1-score of 0.09; this is roughly equivalent to random guessing in the event of a uniform dataset. Classes such as "Public order" and "Shoplifting" acheived high recall, although precision was still low. Broadly, the model collapsed into predicting only a small subset of classes; 4 classes registered an F1-score of 0. The dataset was revisited to enrich the model with more temporal/spatial features, including an interaction term between temporal and spatial features, and the inclusion of frequency encoded LSOA codes. However, the model failed to provide meaningful improvements in predictive performance.

Based on the available temportal and spatial features in the dataset, crime type cannot be reliably predicted.

4 - Random Forest / Gradient Boosting¶

Do predicted crime outcomes differ systematically between rural and urban areas, and do these patterns align with community policing priorities?

4.1 - Assess Model Feasibility¶

Exploratory analysis in Section 2.1 identified substantial class imbalance within recorded crime outcomes. The original multiclass outcome variable contains several infrequently observed categories, which may reduce classification performance and limit the effectiveness of ensemble methods. To address this issue, crime outcomes are reclassified into a binary outcome representing either Resolved or Unresolved cases.

This reclassification introduces important ethical considerations regarding interpretation. A resolved outcome should not be interpreted as evidence of successful policing, victim satisfaction, or positive criminal justice outcomes. Instead, the binary classification reflects whether a recorded investigation reached a formal conclusion according to the official outcome definitions. In the absence of additional contextual information, the published outcome descriptions are treated as the most appropriate ground truth for classification. Thus the analysis evaluates case resolution rather than perceptions of policing effectiveness or sentiment.

4.4 - Conclusion¶

The Random Forest model demonstrates stable predictive performance when classifying binary crime outcomes. F1-scores are highly consistent under 5-fold cross-validation, ranging from 0.706 to 0.711, with a mean F1-score of 0.709. This indicated good generalisability and little evidence of overfitting. The similarity between the cross-validation performance and the independent test-set macro F1-score (0.708) further suggests that the model performs consistently on unseen data.

On the test set, the model achieved an overall accuracy of 71%. Precision and recall were reasonably balanced across both classes, with the model slightly better at identifying unresolved outcomes (recall = 0.74) than resolved outcomes (recall = 0.68). The confusion matrix showed that both classes were predicted successfully without substantial bias towards either outcome, indicating that crime type, temporal, and spatial features contain meaningful information for predicting recorded crime outcomes.

Model performance was evaluated separately on rural and urban subsets to assess whether predictive relationships differ by geography. Results show near-identical performance across both contexts, with rural data achieving a macro F1-score of 0.72 and urban data achieving 0.71. Precision and recall values were also highly consistent across both subsets, indicating that the relationship between crime characteristics and outcome resolution is stable across spatial settings.

These findings suggest that although crime distributions differ between rural and urban areas, the underlying structure governing outcome resolution is broadly consistent. This implies that observed differences in policing outcomes are more likely driven by differences in crime composition rather than fundamentally different outcome-generating processes across geography.

Crime outcome prediction is primarily driven by crime type rather than geographic classification. While rural and urban areas exhibit broadly similar predictive structures, subtle differences exist in the relative importance of specific offence types, with shoplifting being more predictive in urban areas and vehicle crime and burglary showing relatively greater importance in rural contexts. These findings partially align with reported community policing priorities, although the overall effect of geography is limited compared to crime composition. Feature importance values are small and highly skewed due to dominance of a single feature.
